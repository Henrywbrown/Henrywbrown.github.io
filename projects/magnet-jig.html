Variable-Separation Magnet Jig

In Spring 2026 I undertook an independent research project at Wisconsin Lutheran College under Dr. Kerry Kuehn. The physics department had purchased two 2" x 1" N52 neodymium disc magnets in hopes of utilizing them as a fixed magnetic field for various experiments. I was asked to design and built a mechanism that could hold that magnets at a variable separation for experimentation.

Initial Considerations

The disc magnets were purchased from K&J magnetics, their reported strengths when in conjunction with one another are depicted in the force vs distance (in) graph (pictured adjacent). The strength of these magnets poses a number of safety and design challenges. The body of the "jig" or mechanism by which the magnets will be separated must be non-ferrous. The magnets must also be secure so as not to break free from containment. The magnet jig also needed to be convenient for experimentation, having enough space for setups.

Initial Design

I considered three materials for the structure of the jig. First, 316 stainless steel because it is non-magnetic and incredibly strong but its strength also makes it difficult to machine for prototypes. Second, I considered a plastic or an acrylic material; however, considering the strong forces at play and its low yield strength I decided to choose another material. I settled on aluminum, the clear best choice, because it is easy to machine and yet incredibly strong. Since I would be machining my parts, I took the time to learn Fusion 360 in order to model a first prototype. I decided I wanted to use a lead screw and a vise-like construction to achieve a variable separation of the magnets. My first model used a dove-tail bed way with adjustable gibs, a static end, dynamic end, and a lead-screw driving it all. (pictured adjacent)

Updated 3-D Model

While my first model had no actual machining impossibilities, its overall construction was unrealistic. The initial design required a significant amount of material, all of which needed extensive machining. Dr. Kuehn suggested that I consider using materials from 8020.net. I also began browsing hardware and material from McMaster-Carr. I was able to implement both the 8020 materials and McMaster hardware in my updated model. (pictured adjacent)

Building the Jig

With a solid design modeled in Fusion I took the opportunity to make blueprints for all the necessary pieces to aid in the machining process. This project required me to spend a lot of hours learning in the machine shop. My industry experience helped me to pick things up very quickly. I learned to edgefind, peck-drill, frame mill, tap, face, and mill using both linear and circular interpolation. We also optimized the school's verticle bandsaw to be able to cut 1.25" thick 6020 aluminum.

Continued Work

As the project stands on 10/23/26 the lead screw needs to be turned and the dynamic magnet chuck needs its leadscrew threads cut. Once those pieces are completed the jig can be fully assembled and tested. I plan to conduct basic magnetohydrodynamic experiments with the jig and other experimental setups in order to assess the capabilities and or shortfalls of the Variable-Separation Magnet Jig. In the event that the Jig proves successful I will more thoughtfully construct experiments around it. If the jig fails in practical testing I will improve on its design.
